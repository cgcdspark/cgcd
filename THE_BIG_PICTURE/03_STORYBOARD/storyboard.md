### S10 · Generation, grid, transformers

**Narration**

- `05:09.6` First, the power has to be generated. Big Tech is now signing deals to restart nuclear reactors.
- `05:16.3` Microsoft agreed to buy the output of Three Mile Island's Unit One for twenty years, if it comes back online.
- `05:23.6` Gas turbines are in such demand that GE Vernova's gas backlog and reserved production slots have reached one hundred and twenty-two gigawatts.
- `05:33.5` Then the power has to be delivered. And here, the bottleneck is physical.
- `05:38.0` Berkeley Lab found that the typical American power project built in 2025 waited around five years to connect to the grid.
- `05:46.2` And every connection needs transformers. A large power transformer can weigh hundreds of tonnes, and the wait for one is commonly two years or more. For the largest units, up to four.
- `05:57.6` Prices have climbed too: up sixty to eighty percent since 2020, according to Wood Mackenzie.

## Act 6 — Who actually wins?

### S12 · Who actually wins?

**Narration**

- `06:38.9` So who actually wins?
- `06:40.8` The chip designers capture the richest margins today, but they depend on a handful of buyers. Earlier this year, three direct customers accounted for more than half of Nvidia's revenue.
- `06:52.2` The foundries and toolmakers hold positions that are almost impossible to copy, but they must keep investing tens of billions just to stay ahead.
- `07:01.5` The cloud giants carry the biggest bill of all, and investors are watching how much of that spending turns into profit.
- `07:08.5` The grid's equipment makers have something rarer: order books that stretch for years, from customers who cannot wait.
- `07:15.4` And someone has to pay. On America's largest grid, run by PJM, the price of capacity has jumped more than tenfold in three years. Its independent market monitor attributes more than twenty-three billion dollars of added capacity costs to data centre demand. Costs that are passed on to electricity customers.
- `07:36.3` None of this guarantees the future. Backlogs can shrink. Forecasts can be wrong. And capacity built for a boom can sit idle in a downturn.
